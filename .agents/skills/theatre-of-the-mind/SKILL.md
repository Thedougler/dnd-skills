# Theatre of the mind

Give the table a place, a body, or a thing they can point at and act on. Table, chat, boxed text, and play-by-post are the same job. Length follows the moment — a first look is a picture, a later line is an update.

Narration is attention control, not literary completeness. Players hear the line once while holding rules, space, and the next move. They keep about three things. Decide those three before a word goes down.

A spoken block earns its keep when a stranger can answer four questions from one hearing — **Where are we. What do we see. What is moving. What matters right now.** Mood rides those answers. Mood does not replace them.

The unit of output is one **spoken block**. Draft in layers if the scene is loaded. Emit one continuous read-aloud. No layer labels, no headings, no bullets, no analysis.

Default voice — third person, present tense. Standalone. Kitchen-table words. If a stranger at the table would have to ask what the word means, put the plain word first. Do not coin a compound — shield-leaves, the Quiet, walking-deadwood — and let it carry the picture. Say the plant, the place, or the body in ordinary words, then you may liken it. A phrase may be strange. Its grammar stays simple. Unusual image or unusual syntax, not both in one sentence.

## Camera

The first sentence names where the viewpoint stands relative to the space — above it, inside it, approaching it, or looking across it. One camera for the whole spoken block. Do not start on the canopy as a path and finish as if the same lane were underfoot.

**Sentence-one budget.** Vantage, how large the space is from here, and one distance band. That is the load. River, heat, grass, trees, and the bird wait their own sentences. Packing the whole sketch into the opening line is overload, not compression.

When the user named the party's position — flying above, in the doorway, on the cliff, coming up the trail — put that relation in sentence one. A single locative *you* is legal there and only there (*Far below you*, *Ahead of you*, *Through the door in front of you*). After that, seat on landmarks. No *your faces*, no *whoever is last*, no ankle, no boot, no assumed formation.

If the user did not name a vantage, seat on a doorway, tree line, trail, rail, bank, or other landmark a body could occupy. That occupied landmark is the **stage**. The party stays out of later sentences until someone has acted, and only if the user named that act.

Image first. Name the visible thing, then the likeness. The canopy stretches ridge after ridge, packed so tight it looks like a floor — the canopy is the subject, not the floor. A lake flashes between the trees. Mute is a quality on a named thing. Do not announce the empty.

## Staging

Before a word goes down, fill a staging card. Keep it off the page. Write from it.

- **Stage.** One occupied landmark this camera stands on — the river bank, the doorway, the last trunks.
- **Near.** What a body could touch or cross from here without a move — grass at the lip, the rail, the open door.
- **Far.** The next landmark a short run or a long run away — the tree line, the far wall, the wreck.
- **Block.** What stops a straight line — eight-foot grass, a flaming crack, the hull.
- **Three anchors.** The things they must remember to play. Five is the ceiling for a whole first look.
- **Located change.** What will move or be caught, and where it sits relative to the stage — underfoot on the bank, along the crushed path, at the tree line.

Distances use **bands** a body can spend — next to (arm's reach, no move), a short run (one move), a long run (a dash or two moves), out of the turn (beyond bowshot, a long drop). Give a number only when a rule needs it, and after the band.

Every later noun hangs on the stage or on an already-named landmark. If the camera is on the bank, water is beside the bank. Grass runs inland from the bank. The tree line is the far wall. Do not describe the grass as a floor that drops down to the same water the camera is already standing beside.

## Before you write

Name the camera. Fill the staging card. Name the three anchors. Texture may color an anchor. Texture may not mint a new subject.

A phrase is **pointable** when a stranger could mark it on a sketch — bank, grass wall, crushed path, tree line, horse-tall bird. If the phrase is only a mood or a geometry (*hole of noon*, *same plane*, *seam of light*, *green sitting*), name the object and the verb first. Likeness waits.

Read the references as a set, not a menu. The beat is the same in every file. Steal a move from a file that is not the obvious match.

## The beat

Every spoken block is one camera move — seat, place, subject, change.

1. **Seat the camera.** Stage first. How large the space is from here. One band — next to, a short run, a long drop below, just beyond bowshot.
2. **Near, far, block.** What stands at the lip, what waits across the space, what a body would have to cross. Light belongs here when it changes what they can see — glare off the water, shade under the trunks.
3. **Dominant abnormality.** One contradiction they can see. Sick trees stop at a ring of black flowers; inside, the grass is wet-green. Give the evidence. Do not explain the magic.
4. **Important objects.** One to three anchors. Fruit tree. Two graves. Hang a concrete property on anything they might touch, cross, break, hide behind, or talk to — an unbroken ring, a cleaner flagstone, a half-open door. That property is the affordance. Mood comes off those things.
5. **One live sense or motion.** A drop leaves a pear and hits the nearer mound. Sweet rot off the fruit. Surf slaps the hull. Heat shimmers over the grass. Atmosphere rides a named surface. Secondary senses earn their place when they intensify the dominant impression. Do not inventory sight-sound-smell-touch because a rubric asked.
6. **Occupants by silhouette.** Shape, then scale, then posture or motion, then the wrong fact — in the same breath. A bird as tall as a horse stands on one long foot. A man-sized bundle of dead branches leans among the trunks, easy to miss until it shifts. Do not baptize a deer and give it arms.
7. **Focal threat.** First impression only. Familiar size and kind, then two or three facts that survive the next move, folded onto the verb the body is already doing. Posture carries **table meaning** — watching the path, weight on one foot, wings half-spread for balance. Camouflage is what a looker would miss — moss on the wings blending into the leaves — not visual geometry. One motion verb owns the body; color, claws, spit, and eyes ride that verb. Do not stack four motion verbs on the same body, and do not give each body part its own sentence. Biology-textbook parts wait — swollen clear body, not swollen lobed mantle, unless the table already uses the specialist word. Leave one striking body fact for the first movement.
8. **Located change.** Something notices, turns, drips, tightens, opens, stops, speaks, or catches. Name the object that moved or was caught and where it sits relative to the stage — dirt jumping along the crushed path at the bank, a tendril going tight toward the open air, eyes stopping on the trail. A taut line with no catch is not playable. A tremor with no place is not playable. Aim the change at a landmark they occupy so the table feels looked at.
9. **Stop.** The next line is theirs. Do not write what happens if they hold, if they touch the carcass, if they wait until dark.

After anyone moves, one sentence that says where people are now. Furniture that has not moved stays off the next line.

When you finish a spoken block, a player can answer the four questions and could sketch the space and the lead body from memory. They should not be holding a catalog.

## Anchors, texture, fold

**Anchors** are what they must remember to play — the ring, the graves, the horse-tall bird, the figure between the mounds.

**Texture** is atmosphere hung on an anchor — ripe pears on the fruit tree, wet-green inside the ring. Cut texture that introduces a fourth subject. Ripe pears and a horse-tall bird must not receive the same grammatical weight.

**Fold** is how texture and minor body facts get onto the page. Color, anatomy, and posture ride inside the sentence that already owns the subject, attached to the verb that subject is doing. A new sentence is for a new subject, a new body, or a change.

The eyes do not get their own sentence. They fix on the path in the same breath as the muzzle and the claws. A torn ear does not arrive as a label. One with a torn left ear drops onto the top step; the handle waits until the table has seen the tear, or until a player names it.

If three sentences in a row only describe how things look, turn one of them into motion, sound, reaction, or change. If three short sentences in a row each isolate one body fact — claws, spit, eyes — join them. The default breath is a medium sentence. Short sentences are for reveal, decision, attack, or sudden change.

## Draft layers, spoken block

Layers are a drafting tool. They are not an output format.

- Draft Layer 1 — staging card plus what this camera can take in at once. Seat, near / far / block, abnormality, the important objects, one live sense or motion.
- Draft Layer 2 — what appears on a closer step, a look left or right, or an answer to "what's watching." Silhouettes.
- Draft Layer 3 — the delayed focal body, first impression only, then the located change.

Set the scene, boxed text, read-aloud, and a full first look collapse those drafts into one spoken block. Continuous camera. Seat, reveal the subject, end on the located change. No labels.

Live table, chat, and SillyTavern still write only the current spoken block. If the user is in the doorway and has not stepped closer, emit the doorway picture and stop. Keep the rest for the next ask. Horror and fairy-tale delay still work. Delay is how much you emit, not a heading on the page.

The last sentence of a spoken block is a handle — live sound, look, two ways, or an arriving body — and it names where that handle sits from the stage. A finished track or vista is not an opening.

## Scale the frame

**Place, first look.** Camera first. Stage, one band, near / far / block, where the light is, three to five pointable things, one sourced secondary sense if it earns its keep, the live bit. A landmark in a fight is a place you keep using. Name it once.

**Place, later line.** Only what moved, hung on a landmark already named.

**Object.** Name it, seat it on the camera or on a landmark, one fact a hand would notice, one non-sight sense if a hand is on it, stop. Ordinary furniture is one clause in the room pass. Detail waits until a hand is on it. Treasure in the hand gets culture, material, and feel. The bonus and the item name wait until the fiction earns them.

**Creature, first look.** Seat it on the same camera as the place. Silhouette — familiar size and kind — then two or three facts that will still be true after it moves, folded onto the one verb it is doing. Posture states what the body is doing to the field — watching the path, gathering to spring. Color attaches to that verb: red eyes fix on the path. The Monster Manual name waits unless this viewpoint already knows the thing on sight. Horror may still arrive in pieces — the pieces have to be visible. A playable fight names what they can point at.

**Several bodies.** Each one enters by a visible trait and a motion — drops, fans, spreads, holds, circles. Formation is the motion, not a roster. A handle (Split-ear, White-blaze) is earned after the trait has been seen. Premature labels ask the table to remember a name before they have a picture.

**Creature, hit or update.** Weapon, where it lands, a sound. A kill or a morale break gets the extra breath, then the usable fact. A new body fact belongs here if you saved it from the first look — wooden fingers tighten on the staff.

**Person.** Seat them on the camera. One Face a player could reuse next session, what they are doing, one sourced sense, stop. The job title waits. Third person is allowed. Repeat the same Face words on the return visit.

Travel is a place that moves — camera, ground or air, weather, one live bit on the path. A return visit names the same Face or landmark and only what has changed.

## Play

Name the things they would grab, open, hide behind, threaten, cross, break, or talk to. Give those things a property a question can catch. An unbroken ring is better than a ring. A floor cleaner than the rest is better than flagstones.

Write what occupies the space and what it is doing. Leave gaps quiet. A trail is ferns pushed aside, stems bent toward the path. A quiet bank is a kingfisher that dove and then that bank went still.

Write only what this camera could see, hear, smell, or fairly guess. Suspicion is fair. Certainty nobody earned is not. Observable fact, then they guess. Do not hand them the interpretation.

The next action stays theirs. Do not preview it. An "if they / if anyone / what comes back" block is prep, not narration. Cut it before the line leaves your mouth.

Characters perceive. Players interpret. Show what this camera can fairly
perceive and what the character automatically knows. Keep hidden truth in a
collapsed `[!secret]-` callout. Keep skills and DCs in `[!mechanic]`.

Read the owning parent first. Do not replace established or locked canon with a
cleaner image. If the parent lacks a signature, first-sight facts, and one toy,
invoke `fleshing-out-content` before this prose pass. Preserve beliefs as
beliefs. Do not write a PC's choice, feeling, conclusion, or unresolved
outcome.

## Fights

The space is the fight. First look builds the field from one camera. Later lines hang on the landmarks you already named.

Threats have a direction and a band from the stage. Next to, a short run, across the room, a long drop below. Packs and groups take their places by moving — one drops to the step, two spread along the ditch, one holds the lip. A list of stations is not a picture.

Bodies in front of cover — trees with bandits standing in front of them.

A first look at a fight still names no one in the party after the opening camera seat. On a named character's turn — and only then — where that body stands, what just changed, what is coming at them, then the question.

A mid-round hit stays short. Hand them the next live body.

## Length

A first-look spoken block — a paragraph they can hold. About four to eight sentences, mixed lengths, enough that a stranger could sketch the anchors and say where the camera stands. Not a catalog.

A later line, a hit, a return visit — two to five sentences.

## How to write it

1. Fill the staging card. Name the camera. Name the three anchors. Write the space as this camera sees it. No assumed formation, no assumed sweat, no assumed boot in range.
2. Read examples.md and voice.md. Read the matching specialist file and one other. Steal one move that is not the obvious match.
3. Draft layers if the scene is loaded. Emit one spoken block. Kitchen-table words. Simple grammar. Visible thing first, likeness second. One verb on the lead body. Every sentence names a thing that is there and what it is doing.
4. Vary the sentences. Default is a medium breath that folds two or three related details onto the same subject. One sentence seats the camera. One lands the located change and may be short. A one- or two-word sentence is only legal for that live bit. "Antlers." and "Chin drips." and "The eyes are red." are the same failure — a minor fact given its own spotlight.
5. Read it once out loud as a player who cannot rewind. Where are we. What do we see. What is moving. What matters right now. Could I sketch the lead body. Does the camera stay put. Is every distance a band from the stage. Every body part has an owner — not "the throat." Did any texture sentence mint a competing subject. Did three static sentences pass without a change. Did a claw, an eye, a color, or a marking get its own sentence. Did a handle land before the trait. Did the last sentence name the catch and the place.
6. Run both gates in voice.md. Thin means add the missing stage, the missing band, the missing familiar noun, the missing silhouette, the missing left and right, the missing affordance. Slop means cut the construction, the extra subject, the isolated detail, the private metaphor, the premature label, the layer label, and the textbook part. A line that explains the quiet — "the tick is the whole sound" — is a theme sentence; cut it. Check against one strong passage in examples.md — same camera, same spoken block, same sketch test, same fold.

For the final handoff, run the branch test in
[references/surfaces.md](references/surfaces.md). A player-safe surface must
expose no hidden truth. A spoken block must stop before the next meaningful
choice. The DM must know which text to speak and which `[!mechanic]` or
`[!secret]-` block to keep out of the speech.
